# Residue checks

Use this reference when text was machine-drafted or feels more polished than the material behind it. Plain vocabulary, short sentences, and clean grammar produce good prose; residue is what survives them because it is structural rather than lexical. Run these checks before the style pass, because a scannable scaffold and a performed voice both masquerade as good writing.

## Argument self-commentary

The prose describes its own argument instead of making it. The writer rates their material, announces where the reader stands, labels a claim's epistemic status, or interprets a quote before delivering it. This is the most durable tell in otherwise clean copy: no stock phrase, no inflated vocabulary, so a word-level scan returns nothing. It appears most often at paragraph boundaries, where a model reaches for a transition and writes commentary instead.

Four subtypes:

- **Evidence self-rating.** The piece grades its own material: "the cleanest thing we found," "the good part is measurable," "the artifact that convinced me."
- **Structural signposting.** The sentence announces a position rather than advancing one: "then there's the map," "verbosity is only the surface of it," "this sets the boundary on the optimistic version."
- **Epistemic labeling.** The draft names a claim's status instead of making the claim: "the open question is," "two things stay provisional," "one result complicates the picture."
- **Quote characterization.** The draft interprets a quote before delivering it: "Mike put it best," "the complaint was unanimous."

Two tests, either sufficient to flag:

1. **Deletion test.** Cut the sentence. If the argument stands and only reader guidance is lost, it was scaffolding.
2. **Referent test.** Flag nouns pointing at the piece instead of the subject: *the read, the section, the picture, the case, the evidence, the takeaway, the story.* A sentence whose grammatical subject is the piece itself is nearly always commentary on the piece.

The fix is to delete the commentary or convert it into the thing it described. Uncertainty becomes the fact that limits it: "Forty hours isn't long enough to tell," not "the open question is." A turn becomes one word: "also," "either." A quote gets the person and the task, never an interpretation.

Do not flag adjudication. Deciding which of two findings controls a decision is a claim about the world and often required: "Mike's construction check could change the hotel choice. The HTML page could not" stays, while "the construction check is the stronger finding" goes.

## Overcompleted prose

Generated prose tends to complete the material into the most legible possible artifact. It supplies missing causes, regularizes examples, closes open loops, and converts uncertainty into a finished lesson. Prefer a specific unfinished truth to an elegant false one. When notes, transcripts, research, or earlier drafts exist, compare the prose against them and check five failures:

- **Unsupported connective tissue.** Plausibility is not provenance. The most common form is an unwarranted inference: a reason a rule works, a cause behind a number, or a generalization from one case that the source never gives. Details that make the narrative cohere but cannot be traced to the source, motive, scene, sequence, cause, or quoted language, are removed, marked as inference, or sent back as a question.
- **Epistemic inflation.** Observation becomes intention, suspicion becomes diagnosis, possibility becomes mechanism, a partial test becomes a general finding. Restore the source's degree of certainty while keeping precise uncertainty.
- **Causal closure.** One cause explains every example, every anecdote supports the thesis, or a concluding equation compresses a tradeoff the evidence has not settled. Separate what happened from what the writer thinks it may mean, and let examples disagree when they genuinely disagree.
- **Framework compliance.** Sections of suspiciously equal length with identical internal logic, every example receiving the same diagnosis, or a taxonomy that absorbs all exceptions. Let the material determine the structure rather than the framework driving it.
- **Decorative research.** Studies that arrive after the interpretation is already settled, or evidence that supplies borrowed authority while changing no judgment. Name the source, state the finding plainly, attach the material limit, and cut research that changes nothing.

When no source is available, these become questions rather than assertions: is this motive established or inferred, do these examples actually share one cause, would the writer endorse this degree of certainty?

## Portable profundity

Apply the portability test: could the line move into an unrelated essay or social post with only the topic nouns changed? Common forms are polished causal equations ("X without Y is just Z"), universal declarations after a single example, inversions written to be quoted, and final lines that restate the paragraph at a higher register.

Either reattach the sentence to the named person, artifact, choice, or consequence that earned it, or cut it. If specificity destroys the line's force, the shape was doing the work.

Run the same test on the whole piece: could the reader have produced this text by asking a chatbot about the topic, without the writer's material? If so, the piece has not yet used what only the writer knows (the numbers, the incident, the decision, the failure). Rebuild it around that material, or say plainly that the writer has no particular view.

Two more forms of overreach to flag: a vision or trend claimed from a single case ("this is clearly where the industry is going"), whose scope must shrink to its sample; and a headline number that belongs to a different object than the claim (the old system's cost cited for the new one).

## Warm-up and arrival tracks

The reader should experience the destination, not the journey the writer took to find it. Revision also leaves residue: earlier versions show through as phantom structure. Look for:

- **The warm-up.** The real opening is buried in paragraph three or four. Start the piece where it lands.
- **The arrival sequence.** "After much research, I realized," "it took me a while to see." Start where you arrived; the reader does not need the itinerary.
- **Discovery narration.** "Surprisingly," "I didn't expect to find," "what I learned was." The reader experiences the discovery through the idea itself, not by being told you discovered it.
- **Thinking out loud.** "This raises the question," "one might wonder." The question has already been answered; give the answer.
- **The hedge trail.** Hedges multiply during revision. Keep the uncertainty the argument still needs; cut the ones that survived from an earlier draft.
- **The deleted argument's ghost.** A rebuttal to a point no longer in the piece, or a transition to a section that was cut. Amputate them.
- **Restated lessons.** A closing "Lessons learned" list, a "To sum up" box after each section, or a pull quote that repeats a body sentence word for word. Keep only a lesson the body did not already state.
- **Belt and suspenders.** The same point made twice in different words because the first version was not trusted: a tautology. Fix the first version instead of repeating it.

For each suspect passage ask: is this here for the reader or for the writer, is this the thought or the process of thinking it, and would the argument stand without it?

## Guardrails

Not every match is a problem. Preserve these:

- Adjudication between findings, which is a claim; describing that adjudication, which is scaffolding.
- Precise domain terms, even when they appear on common tell lists.
- Warranted, precise uncertainty; cut only reflexive or vague hedging.
- Intentional irony, quotation, and voice moves that carry the writer's point of view but stay sparse, one conspicuous move to a paragraph at most.
- Kept errors, disorder, or jargon are never added back to make prose seem human; do not manufacture quirky specificity as an antidote to polished generic prose. Specificity must come from the material.
