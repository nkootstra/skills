# Adaptation guide

## Choose by purpose

### Explanation

Lead with the phenomenon or question. Build a mental model, demonstrate it with a concrete case, then state the implication. Spend more space on mechanism than on claims of significance.

### Tutorial

State the outcome and prerequisites early. Give the reader a model of what the steps change, not only commands. Organize actions in dependency order. Include checkpoints, common failures, and a clear finished state.

### Opinion or argument

State a contestable, bounded position. Establish shared facts or goals, develop the strongest reasons, treat counterarguments seriously, and end with the judgment the evidence supports.

### Product or feature explanation

Begin with the user's situation or changed capability. Explain how it works at the depth needed to trust it. Connect features to behavior and constraints. Avoid promotional adjectives, invented user enthusiasm, and long capability inventories.

### Retrospective or case study

Distinguish what was known at the time from what became clear later. Explain why earlier choices were reasonable, identify the evidence that changed the team's view, and extract lessons without pretending every outcome was predictable.

### Original research or data report

Every item below is a test the report must pass:

- **Scope line first.** What the data covers, what it cannot see, and who the findings describe.
- **Finding headings.** Each heading states the finding with its number ("Time to first call fell from 11 days to 4"), not the topic.
- **A baseline for every number.** Each headline number names its baseline, cohort, or time window, and sample sizes appear wherever a fragment could pass for a population.
- **The limitation next to its finding.** When a metric flatters the result (an exclusion, a survivor cohort, a favorable window), say so in the paragraph that presents the finding, then say why the finding still holds. Not only in the methodology.
- **Claims scaled to the metric.** Correlation stays correlation; motion is not value; "apparent driver" stays apparent.
- **A Methodology section.** A heading of its own, after the findings, listing data sources, windows, exclusions, and definitions, each with the reason for the choice.
- **Judgment at the end.** Section prose stays close to the data; the interpretation goes in one closing section, no stronger than the notes.

### Narrative or profile

Use scenes and chronology selectively. Let concrete action reveal the larger idea, then move into interpretation without over-explaining every detail. Preserve ambiguity when it is part of the truth.

### Announcement or update

Lead with what changed and who it affects. Explain why, state timing and required action, then supply relevant context. Keep celebration proportional to the news.

## Choose by length

### Very short: one paragraph to roughly 250 words

Use one governing move and little scaffolding. Open directly, make or explain the point, support it with the strongest available detail, and finish on the consequence. Usually omit headings and formal summaries.

### Short: roughly 250–800 words

Use two to four movements. A single example can carry the piece. Limit background and objections to what changes the conclusion. Use headings only when they improve scanning.

### Medium: roughly 800–2,000 words

Make the structural spine visible. Develop the mechanism or argument across purposeful sections, vary pace, and include meaningful evidence or objections. Reorient the reader at genuine turns without repeatedly summarizing.

### Long: more than roughly 2,000 words

Establish a hierarchy of claims. Give sections distinct jobs and order them by conceptual dependency. Use multiple examples only when they test different boundaries. Track terminology and claims for consistency. Earn the ending by synthesizing rather than recapping.

Treat word counts as calibration, not law.

## Choose by audience

### General audience

Begin from observable experience. Define necessary terms in place, minimize prerequisite chains, and use examples with familiar consequences. Preserve complexity through explanation rather than jargon.

### Practitioner audience

Assume common foundations but state consequential assumptions. Focus on mechanisms, tradeoffs, edge cases, and decisions. Use domain terms when they make the prose more precise.

### Expert audience

Move quickly to the disputed or non-obvious point. Specify scope, evidence, and uncertainty. Do not waste space reteaching foundations, but do not hide a weak inference behind specialist language.

### Mixed audience

Keep the main line readable and layer technical depth. Put the essential explanation in prose; isolate optional implementation detail in examples, notes, diagrams, or appendices when the format allows.

## Adapt revisions

For an original draft, choose the structure before composing. For a rewrite, identify the source's real claim and preserve distinctive phrasing that supports it. For a light edit, correct clarity and rhythm without silently changing conclusions, certainty, or personality. For house-style work, apply explicit house rules before these defaults.
